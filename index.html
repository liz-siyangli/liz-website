New York · October 2025

An urgent trip to a sold-out convention — and a single offhand comment that became a product.

By October 2025, Good Humans had moved fast. We'd closed a round and were entering real production — not prototype, not lab, factory. Our manufacturing co-founder needed to fly to China to oversee the first production run in person. But before she left, we had a problem: we didn't have enough direct user feedback to tell her what she'd be asking the factory to build. The time window was under a week.

I found the right room: a Love and Deep Space fan event in New York — a birthday celebration for the character Xavier, the biggest gathering of its kind on the East Coast, drawing fans from Philly, Boston, up and down the coast. Vendor slots had been closed for months. I reached out anyway. Explained who we were, what we were building, why our presence would matter to the people in that room. We got added to a sold-out lineup.

For seven days I ran interviews everywhere — in the coffee queue before doors opened, on the show floor, in a private room I unlocked after buying the sign-in volunteer a coffee. Our manufacturing co-founder flew in. She sat across from the people who would become our first customers and got the direct answers she'd be bringing to the factory.

In one of those conversations, a user mentioned she wanted her AI companion to wear matching outfits. I didn't let it go. I heard variations of it again. I ran a Discord poll — the signal held. I brought it to the team formally. The co-founders pushed back: we're an AI startup from San Francisco, this feels too low-tech. I showed them the data. Matching Outfit launched.

500 new community members from this event alone — highest purchase-intent cohort we tracked
70%+ of Batch 2 buyers added Matching Outfit at $100+ · every restock since has sold out in under 5 min

San Francisco · December 2025

Funding Season. Fifteen days to build the pre-sale. Five minutes to sell out.

December 9th. Pre-sale: December 24th. We were in the middle of Funding Season — investors were watching, and this launch would be part of the story they were evaluating. It needed to go well. Not just okay. Well.

I mapped out a full content calendar before touching the camera. Thirty videos in fifteen days — roughly two a day — each with a call to action calibrated to how many days remained before launch. I coordinated daily with our designer on countdown visuals. I synced with our manufacturing co-founder in China for the latest footage from the factory floor. And while doing all of that, I noticed no one had built the Sold Out page. I built it before anyone asked.

December 24th. San Francisco. 5pm PST. The office had lost power earlier that day. We drove through the rain looking for a café with wifi. Found one. Opened the backend. Watched. Five minutes. All 100 units gone. Discord exploded — thousands of messages in those same five minutes. People saying what they bought, what outfit they'd pair it with, where they'd put it in their apartment.

5 min to sell 100 units on Dec 24 · investors saw the data the same night
30 videos in 15 days · scripted, shot, edited, published, and tracked — solo

Leaving the startup
didn't mean leaving
the work.

I'm curious in the literal sense — I want to understand how things actually work, not just the surface version. I notice details that don't always matter until they suddenly do. I fix things around me without being asked, because leaving something broken feels worse than the effort. And I've been working with AI long enough that it's not a tool I pick up — it's just how I think.

To finish my Master's in Computer Science at Northeastern, I had to leave Good Humans and go back to school. Northeastern's San Jose campus made it possible to move to Stanford's campus and live with family at the same time. Leaving a startup is a real gear shift — at a company like that, staying current isn't something you have to try to do, the work forces it. As a student again, that external pressure disappears. I didn't let it matter. I kept building AI projects on my own, joined vibe coding workshops and AI classes on campus, and showed up to lectures and seminars every week.

"Take the play seriously and get my hands dirty."

After leaving Good Humans, I required myself to spend at least one hour a day on this. Not for a job. Not for a grade. Just because I believe — based on my CS background and everything I built at the company — that AI is going to change what productivity actually means. And I didn't want to drift away from that just because I wasn't on a payroll.

The way I see it: you can't learn what AI can do right now by reading about it. The field changes faster than any syllabus. You can't rely on a course that's been taught for twenty years, even by someone excellent. The only way to actually understand where the models are — and where they're not — is to explore in a non-utilitarian way. No target DAU. No revenue goal. Just: what happens if I do it this way? Can this actually be solved without a person in the loop? What's at the edge? I try something, see what breaks, adjust, try again. That's the practice. That's how I stay current on my own terms.
