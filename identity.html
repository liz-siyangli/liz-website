The background that shaped how I work. Open Close

I was the first employee at Good Humans, an AI hardware startup that went from accelerator to seed funding in under six months. With no playbook and no team, I built the company's marketing engine, operations infrastructure, and growth systems from scratch.

I replaced repetitive work with AI agents, LLM pipelines, and custom internal tooling; shipped two consumer features end-to-end; ran 500+ user surveys and interviews; and helped grow a community of 3,500+ while the role was still being defined around me.

Before tech, I spent five years at Beijing University of Chinese Medicine, including two years of clinical training. That background is not a detour. It is part of why I think carefully about systems, constraints, and what happens when decisions have real consequences.

Jan – Jun 2026 · Back to school, still building

Leaving the startup
didn't mean leaving
the work.

Leaving Good Humans was practical, not emotional. I needed to finish my Master's in Computer Science, so I stepped out of the company and went back to being a student. What changed most was the rhythm. At a startup, the urgency is built in: customers ask, the team needs something, something breaks, and you move. Once I was back in school, no one was pushing me in that same way.

After I left, I moved to Stanford campus while finishing my Northeastern coursework, and I did not want that muscle to go quiet. Being there gave me access to coding workshops, AI lectures, product talks, and small conversations that kept me close to what people were actually building and debating. I learned a lot from that environment, but the important part was that I kept turning it back into practice: build something, test a workflow, take notes, and try it again in my own projects.

"Take the play seriously and get my hands dirty."

After leaving Good Humans, I asked myself to spend at least one hour a day staying close to the work. Not for a class. Not for a resume bullet. Mostly because I had already seen how quickly AI changes what one person can build, and I did not want to lose that instinct just because I was no longer inside a startup every day.

The way I see it, AI is not something you can understand from a distance. The useful questions only show up when you try to use it: can this task become a workflow, where does the model fail, what still needs human judgment, what can be automated without making the result worse? I try something, see what breaks, adjust, and try again. That is how I stay current on my own terms.
